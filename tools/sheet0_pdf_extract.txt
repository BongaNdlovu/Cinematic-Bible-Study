THE	BATTLE	OF	HERMENEUTICS
Daniel	does	not	begin	with	a	timeline.
He	begins	with	a	crisis.
Judah	is	under	imperial	pressure.	Daniel	is	taken	from	his	homeland	to	Babylon	(Daniel	1:3–6).
Jerusalem	has	been	humbled	by	siege.	And	somewhere	inside	the	machinery	of	Babylon,	God	is
preparing	to	reveal	something	far	larger	than	the	rise	and	fall	of	one	empire.
Daniel	will	see	kingdoms	rise.	He	will	see	kingdoms	fall.	He	will	see	rulers	exalt	themselves.	He	will
see	earthly	powers	attempt	to	control	history.	And	eventually,	he	will	see	a	kingdom	that	does	not
pass	away.
But	before	we	trace	the	kingdoms,	we	need	to	answer	a	more	fundamental	question:	How	should
prophecy	be	interpreted?
This	is	the	question	of	hermeneutics.	Hermeneutics	is	the	discipline	of	interpreting	texts.	In	prophetic
study,	it	asks	questions	such	as:
What	does	the	biblical	text	actually	say?
What	does	the	historical	setting	tell	us?
How	should	symbols	be	interpreted?
How	do	different	passages	illuminate	one	another?
How	does	
Scripture	interpret	Scripture
	—	so	that	clearer	passages	guard	the	harder	ones?
When	does	an	interpretation	move	beyond	the	explicit	wording	of	Scripture?
How	should	historical	evidence	be	used?
And	ultimately,	how	does	prophecy	point	us	toward	Christ?
This	lesson	establishes	the	interpretive	framework	that	will	guide	our	journey	through	Daniel	—	and
it	will	show,	from	Bible	study,	
why	the	historicist	reading	best	accounts	for	Daniel's	own
evidence
.	We	still	label	Text,	History,	and	Interpretation	honestly.	Honesty	is	not	neutrality.	The
point	is	to	prove	the	case,	not	to	leave	the	reader	stranded	between	equal	options.

PART	I	—	DANIEL	ENTERS	HISTORY
Jerusalem	Under	Babylonian	Pressure
Daniel	1:1–2
"In	the	third	year	of	the	reign	of	Jehoiakim	king	of	Judah	came	Nebuchadnezzar	king	of	Babylon
unto	Jerusalem,	and	besieged	it."
Daniel	does	not	begin	in	a	mythical	world.	He	begins	in	a	recognizable	historical	setting.	There	is	a
king.	There	is	a	city.	There	is	an	invading	empire.	There	is	a	siege.	And	there	is	a	transfer	of	people
and	sacred	vessels	from	Jerusalem	to	Babylon.
Daniel	1:2	adds	something	theologically	significant:
"And	the	Lord	gave	Jehoiakim	king	of	Judah	into	his	hand…"
The	political	catastrophe	is	presented	within	the	sovereignty	of	God.	The	text	does	not	merely	say
that	Babylon	was	stronger.	It	says	that	the	Lord	gave	Jehoiakim	into	Nebuchadnezzar's	hand.
That	becomes	an	important	theme	throughout	Daniel:	Earthly	kingdoms	operate	within	a	history	that
ultimately	remains	under	God's	sovereignty.
HISTORICAL	NOTE
Daniel	identifies	the	beginning	of	his	Babylonian	experience	by	the	third	year	of	Jehoiakim.
Traditional	chronological	reconstructions	place	the	first	Babylonian	incursion	associated	with	Daniel
around	605	B.C.,	in	connection	with	the	battle	at	Carchemish,	where	Egyptian	forces	were	decisively
defeated.	At	the	time	of	that	battle	Nebuchadnezzar	was	still	crown	prince,	commanding	the	army	on
his	father	Nabopolassar's	behalf;	Nabopolassar	died	shortly	afterward	(mid-August	605	B.C.),	and
Nebuchadnezzar	took	the	Babylonian	throne	himself	only	a	few	weeks	later,	in	early	September.
Daniel	1:1	calls	him	"king	of	Babylon"	in	a	way	many	readers	take	
proleptically
	—	by	the	title	he
held	by	the	time	of	writing,	not	necessarily	the	title	he	held	at	the	exact	moment	of	the	siege.	That
reading	is	a	historical-literary	reconstruction,	not	something	Daniel	1	spells	out	in	those	words.
The	precise	BCE	date	is	a	reconstruction	based	on	biblical	and	ancient	chronological	evidence	rather
than	a	date	explicitly	written	in	Daniel	1.
This	distinction	matters	—	and	so	does	a	related	one.	Jeremiah	dates	the	same	events	to	Jehoiakim's
fourth	year,	not	the	third	(Jeremiah	25:1;	46:2),	which	will	be	one	of	the	Source	Checkpoints	below.
A	standard	scholarly	resolution
	is	that	Daniel	is	using	Babylonian	accession-year	reckoning	—
where	a	king's	first	full	regnal	year	is	counted	from	the	next	New	Year's	day	after	he	takes	the	throne,
not	from	his	accession	itself	—	while	Jeremiah	is	using	a	Judean	non-accession-year	system	common
in	the	west.	Other	calendar	models	also	exist.	Both	writers	can	be	counting	correctly	and	still	land	on
different	numbers,	because	they	are	using	different	calendars	to	do	the	counting.	This	is	a	good	early

TEXT
Daniel	tells	us:

HISTORY
Ancient	Near	Eastern	chronology	and	Babylonian	records	provide	external	evidence	for	the

Babylonian	conquest	of	Judah.
INTERPRETATION
The	historicist	reading	sees	Daniel's	experience	as	the	opening	scene	of	a	prophetic	narrative	in	which
God	reveals	a	sequence	of	earthly	kingdoms	leading	toward	His	everlasting	kingdom.
CHRIST
The	biblical	story	begins	with	God's	people	apparently	defeated.	But	Daniel's	book	will	repeatedly
demonstrate	that	apparent	defeat	does	not	mean	divine	absence.	That	theme	ultimately	finds	its	fullest
expression	in	Christ.
PART	II	—	WHAT	DOES	"HERMENEUTICS"	MEAN?
Before	We	Interpret	Daniel,	We	Need	to	Know	How	We	Are	Reading	Daniel
Imagine	three	people	standing	before	the	same	ancient	map.	All	three	see	the	same	rivers.	All	three
see	the	same	mountains.	All	three	see	the	same	cities.	But	each	person	has	been	given	a	different	set
of	assumptions	about	what	the	map	represents.	They	may	reach	different	conclusions	without
changing	a	single	line	on	the	map.
Prophetic	interpretation	works	similarly.	The	biblical	text	is	one	thing.	Our	method	of	interpreting
that	text	is	another.	That	is	why	hermeneutics	matters.
THE	FOUR	LEVELS	OF	THIS	COURSE
Throughout	this	study,	we	will	use	four	clearly	distinguished	levels.

LEVEL	3	—	INTERPRETATION
What	conclusion	follows	from	the	framework	being	used?	This	is	where	hermeneutics	becomes

A	historicist	interpretation	may	connect	a	prophetic	symbol	with	a	sequence	of	historical	events.	A

Throughout	this	course,	we	will	identify	the	historicist	reading	clearly	rather	than	presenting	it	as
though	every	conclusion	were	explicitly	stated	by	the	biblical	text.

PART	III	—	THE	HISTORICIST	FRAMEWORK
What	Is	Historicism?
Historicism	is	an	interpretive	framework	that	understands	biblical	prophecy	as	unfolding
progressively	through	history.	In	prophetic	passages	containing	sequences	of	kingdoms,	powers,
conflicts,	and	time	periods,	historicist	interpreters	seek	to	trace	those	symbols	through	successive
historical	developments.
The	central	idea	is	simple:	Prophecy	can	describe	a	sequence	of	events	extending	across	the	course	of
history	rather	than	being	confined	entirely	to	the	prophet's	immediate	generation	or	exclusively	to	a
final	future	crisis.
This	course	will	use	that	framework	when	interpreting	Daniel.	But	there	is	an	important	distinction:
Historicism	is	a	framework	of	interpretation.	The	Bible	does	not	use	the	word	historicism	to	describe
itself.	Therefore,	rather	than	claiming	"Historicism	is	the	biblical	method,"	we	will	ask	"How	does	the
historicist	framework	understand	the	biblical	evidence?"	That	distinction	allows	us	to	study	the
subject	honestly.	Later	in	this	sheet	(Part	IX)	we	will	not	stop	at	definition.	We	will	prove	from
Daniel	and	the	wider	canon	why	historicism	is	the	correct	framework	for	this	course.
DANIEL	2	—	THE	FIRST	MAJOR	TEST
Daniel	2	gives	us	a	remarkable	sequence.	Nebuchadnezzar	sees	a	great	image.	Daniel	explains	that
the	image	represents	kingdoms.	The	first	kingdom	is	explicitly	identified:	"Thou	art	this	head	of
gold."	Babylon.
Then	Daniel	describes	what	follows:	"And	after	thee	shall	arise	another	kingdom	inferior	to	thee…"
Then	another.	Then	another.	And	finally	comes	the	kingdom	represented	by	the	stone.
Daniel	2	therefore	unquestionably	contains	a	sequence.	The	major	interpretive	question	is	not
whether	the	sequence	exists.	It	does.	The	question	is:	How	far	does	that	sequence	extend,	and	how
should	its	symbols	and	historical	fulfillments	be	identified?
Historicists	see	Daniel	2	as	a	foundational	example	of	prophecy	unfolding	through	successive
historical	kingdoms.	Other	interpretive	approaches	also	recognize	the	sequence	of	kingdoms.	The
disagreement	concerns	how	the	sequence	should	be	extended	and	fulfilled	—	and	even	the	identity	of

the	second	kingdom	is	not	entirely	uncontested:	most	historicist	and	confessional	readings	take	it	as
Medo-Persia	as	a	single	power,	while	some	critical	scholarship	divides	it	into	separate	Median	and

PART	IV	—	HISTORICIST	INTERPRETATION	IN	CHRISTIAN
HISTORY
Interpreters	Before	and	During	the	Reformation
Historicist	readings	of	prophecy	did	not	suddenly	appear	in	the	nineteenth	century.	Various	Christian
interpreters	in	the	medieval	and	Reformation	eras	developed	interpretations	that	understood	prophetic
passages	as	having	historical	fulfillment	extending	across	long	periods	of	Christian	history.
Among	interpreters	associated	with	historicist-style	readings	
before	and	during
	the	Reformation
were	figures	such	as:
John	Wycliffe	(died	1384	—	a	precursor	before	the	Protestant	Reformation)
Martin	Luther
Philipp	Melanchthon
John	Knox
However,	their	interpretations	were	not	identical.	They	differed	on	chronology,	symbols,	prophetic
identification,	and	details	of	fulfillment.	Therefore,	it	is	more	accurate	to	speak	of	historicist
tendencies
	and	readings	among	major	Christian	interpreters	rather	than	claiming	that	every	Reformer
held	one	uniform	prophetic	system.
Some	interpreters	also	connected	prophetic	passages	such	as	Daniel	7	and	Revelation	13	with	the
long	history	of	ecclesiastical	and	political	power.	These	interpretations	contributed	to	the
development	of	historicist	Protestant	readings	of	prophecy.
PRIMARY	SOURCE	CHECKPOINT	—	Martin	Luther
Luther's	writings	demonstrate	that	prophetic	passages	concerning	antichrist	and	ecclesiastical	power
were	interpreted	historically	within	portions	of	the	Reformation	tradition.	This	is	important	evidence
for	the	history	of	interpretation.	But	historical	evidence	about	Luther	does	not	prove	that	every	detail
of	the	historicist	framework	is	correct.	It	demonstrates	something	narrower:	historicist-style
interpretations	existed	prominently	within	the	Reformation	era.

--- PAGE 7 ---
ISAAC	NEWTON	—	A	Later	Historicist
Isaac	Newton	studied	biblical	prophecy	extensively,	roughly	a	century	after	the	Reformation	era
proper.	His	writings	demonstrate	that	a	serious	engagement	with	Daniel	and	Revelation	could	involve
historical	sequence,	symbolism,	chronology,	and	comparison	between	prophetic	passages.	He	is
grouped	here	not	as	a	Reformer	but	as	evidence	that	the	historicist	approach	continued	to	attract
serious	minds	well	past	the	sixteenth	century.	Again,	the	purpose	of	these	sources	is	not	to	establish
Newton	as	an	authority	over	Scripture.	They	show	how	influential	Christian	interpreters	approached
prophecy	historically.
HISTORY
	—	These	sources	belong	to	the	history	of	interpretation.
INTERPRETATION
	—	They	demonstrate	that	historical	approaches	to	prophecy	have	a	long
Christian	history.
TEXT
	—	They	do	not	replace	the	biblical	text.
PART	V	—	THE	COUNTER-REFORMATION	AND	ALTERNATIVE
INTERPRETIVE	SYSTEMS
Be	direct	about	the	history.
The	Reformation	stake:	historicism	and	the	papacy
Historicist	Protestant	readers	did	not	treat	prophecy	as	a	harmless	hobby.	Using	Scripture	with
Scripture	—	Daniel's	beasts	and	little	horn,	Paul's	man	of	sin,	and	Revelation's	beast	and	Babylon	—
they	identified	the	
papal	system
	with	the	prophetic	Antichrist	/	beast	power	opposing	Christ	and	His
truth	across	history.
That	identification	was	public	and	confessional	in	broad	Reformation	streams	(for	example	in
Luther's	writings	on	Antichrist	and	in	related	Protestant	testimony).	It	hurt	Rome's	claim	to
unquestioned	spiritual	authority.	So	the	prophetic	question	became	a	Counter-Reformation	battlefield.
TEXT	/	SCRIPTURE-WITH-SCRIPTURE	(the	historicist	claim):
	Daniel	7;	2	Thessalonians	2;
Revelation	13;	17	—	a	dynastic,	historical	Antichrist	power,	not	merely	one	brief	ancient	tyrant	and
not	merely	one	future	individual	disconnected	from	the	medieval–modern	story.
HISTORY:
	Reformers	preached	and	wrote	that	identification	openly.
Rome's	two	answers:	shove	prophecy	into	the	past,	or	shove	it	into	a	tiny	future

relocate	the	fulfillment

THREE	MAJOR	FRAMEWORKS
HISTORICISM
	—	Prophetic	fulfillment	unfolds	progressively	through	history.	A	symbolic
sequence	may	correspond	to	successive	historical	kingdoms,	institutions,	conflicts,	and	events.
PRETERISM
	—	Preterist	approaches	generally	locate	major	prophetic	fulfillment	primarily	within
the	ancient	historical	setting.	There	are	different	forms	of	preterism,	and	they	do	not	all	interpret
every	prophetic	passage	in	exactly	the	same	way.
FUTURISM
	—	Futurist	approaches	understand	significant	portions	of	biblical	prophecy	as	awaiting
fulfillment	in	the	future.	Again,	futurism	contains	different	forms	and	should	not	be	reduced	to	a
single	system.
THE	QUESTION	IS	NOT:
	"Which	system	sounds	most	dramatic?"
The	real	question	is:
	Which	interpretation	best	accounts	for	the	biblical	text	when	Scripture
interprets	Scripture	—	and	which	systems	were	built,	historically,	to	escape	the	historicist
identification	of	the	papacy	with	the	beast?
This	course	answers	both:	historicism	fits	Daniel;	and	the	Counter-Reformation	alternatives	were
forged	as	escapes	from	that	conclusion.
PART	VI	—	THE	YEAR-DAY	QUESTION
One	of	the	most	important	interpretive	questions	in	historicist	prophecy	concerns	prophetic	time.
Why	do	historicist	interpreters	understand	certain	prophetic	"days"	as	representing	years?
The	answer	begins	with	Scripture	—	though	it's	worth	flagging	up	front	that	neither	of	the	two	texts
usually	cited	is	itself	predictive	prophecy.	Both	are	retrospective	judgment	declarations	or	symbolic
sign-acts.	What	they	establish	is	that	God	can	and	does	use	a	day-for-year	relationship	in	His	dealings
with	His	people;	whether	that	same	relationship	should	be	imported	into	Daniel's	predictive	time
periods	is	a	separate	question,	answered	by	examining	Daniel's	own	context	—	not	by	these	two	texts
alone.
NUMBERS	14:34
Israel's	spies	explored	Canaan	for	forty	days.	After	Israel	rejected	God's	promise,	God	declared:
"After	the	number	of	the	days	in	which	ye	searched	the	land,	even	forty	days,	each	day	for	a	year…"
Here	Scripture	explicitly	establishes	a	relationship	between	40	days	and	40	years.	This	occurs	within
a	specific	prophetic	judgment,	announced	after	the	fact	rather	than	as	a	forward-looking	prediction
using	symbolic	time.
EZEKIEL	4:6
God	tells	Ezekiel:	"I	have	appointed	thee	each	day	for	a	year."
Again,	Scripture	explicitly	establishes:	one	day,	one	year.	This	is	a	symbolic	sign-act	Ezekiel	is
commanded	to	perform,	not	itself	a	prophecy	stated	in	coded	time.

--- PAGE 10 ---
These	passages	demonstrate	that	God	can	use	a	day-for-year	relationship	in	prophetic	symbolism.	But
we	must	not	make	an	invalid	leap.	They	do	not	establish	a	universal	rule	that	every	prophetic	day	in
every	biblical	passage	must	equal	one	year.	Instead,	historicist	interpreters	argue	that	these	passages
provide	biblical	precedent	for	symbolic	time	and	then	examine	whether	the	context	of	Daniel
supports	applying	that	principle	there.	That	is	the	proper	methodological	sequence.
DANIEL'S	LONG	TIME	PERIODS
Daniel	itself	contains	several	unusually	large	or	unusual	prophetic	time	expressions.	Among	them
are:
"a	time,	times,	and	the	dividing	of	time"	(Daniel	7:25;	12:7)
2,300	evenings	and	mornings	(Daniel	8:14)
70	weeks	(Daniel	9:24)
1,290	days	(Daniel	12:11)
1,335	days	(Daniel	12:12)
Historicist	interpreters	understand	these	periods	symbolically	and	apply	the	day-year	principle.	Under
that	framework:
70	weeks	→	70	×	7	=	490	prophetic	units	→	applied	through	the	historicist	day-year
interpretation	→	490	years
2,300	evenings	and	mornings	→	applied	through	the	historicist	framework	→	2,300	years
"a	time,	times,	and	half	a	time"	→	generally	read	as	3½	prophetic	years	of	360	days	each	→	1,260
prophetic	days	→	applied	through	the	day-year	framework	→	1,260	years
That	last	figure	—	1,260	—	is	worth	flagging	carefully,	because	it	does	not	appear	as	a	number	in
Daniel	itself.	It	is	arrived	at	by	combining	two	further	interpretive	steps	on	top	of	the	day-year
principle:	reading	"time,	times,	and	half	a	time"	as	3½	years,	and	then	converting	that	to	days	using	a
360-day	prophetic-calendar	year.	The	number	1,260	appears	explicitly	only	in	Revelation	(11:3;
12:6),	alongside	"forty-two	months"	(11:2;	13:5),	which	is	the	same	period	stated	a	different	way.	So
the	reasoning	runs:	Daniel's	phrase	→	3½	years	(interpretation)	→	1,260	days	(calendar	convention)
→	1,260	years	(day-year	principle)	→	correlated	with	Revelation's	parallel	figure.	Four	layers,	not
one	—	and	it's	important	that	a	student	can	see	all	four	rather	than	encountering	1,260	as	though
Daniel	stated	it	outright.
The	distinction	remains	essential:	the	numbers	490	and	2,300	are	Daniel's	own;	the	day-year
application	is	an	interpretation;	and	1,260	specifically	is	a	further	downstream	construction	built	on
that	interpretation	plus	a	chronological	convention.

--- PAGE 11 ---
DANIEL	9	AS	A	SUPPORTING	TEST
Historicist	interpreters	see	Daniel	9	as	an	important	internal	test	of	the	prophetic-time	framework.
Daniel	receives	a	prophecy	of	"seventy	weeks."	The	prophecy	discusses	Jerusalem,	restoration,
Messiah	(the	anointed	one),	transgression,	reconciliation,	the	end	of	sin,	and	the	destruction	of
Jerusalem.
If	the	seventy	weeks	were	interpreted	as	merely	490	literal	days,	the	chronology	would	not
correspond	naturally	to	the	historical	sequence	described	in	the	passage.	Historicist	interpreters
therefore	apply	the	day-year	principle	and	understand	the	period	as	490	years.	They	then	compare
that	interpretation	with	Persian-period	chronology	and	the	New	Testament's	chronology	surrounding
Jesus.	This	produces	the	familiar	historicist	chronology	associated	with	457	B.C.,	A.D.	27,	A.D.	31,
and	A.D.	34.
But	these	dates	must	be	demonstrated	carefully.	They	are	not	simply	written	in	Daniel	as	modern
calendar	dates.	They	result	from	a	chronological	and	interpretive	argument	that	we	will	examine	in
later	lessons.
IMPORTANT	WARNING
Do	not	use	Daniel	9	to	prove	the	day-year	principle	and	then	use	the	day-year	principle	to	prove
Daniel	9	without	acknowledging	the	circularity.	Instead:
1.	
Establish	that	Scripture	sometimes	uses	day-for-year	symbolism.
2.	
Examine	Daniel's	language	and	context.
3.	
Apply	the	proposed	interpretation.
4.	
Compare	the	resulting	chronology	with	independent	historical	and	biblical	evidence.
5.	
Ask	whether	the	interpretation	explains	the	evidence	coherently.
That	is	how	we	test	an	interpretation.
PART	VII	—	THE	2,300	EVENINGS	AND	MORNINGS
Daniel	8:14	says:	"Unto	two	thousand	and	three	hundred	days;	then	shall	the	sanctuary	be	cleansed."
This	passage	is	central	to	historicist	sanctuary	interpretation.	But	it	requires	careful	handling.	The
underlying	Hebrew	literally	reads	"evening-morning"	(
ereb	boqer
),	an	idiom	most	translations	render
either	as	"2,300	days"	(following	the	standard	pattern	of	counting	evening-and-morning	as	one	day,
as	in	Genesis	1)	or	as	"2,300	evenings	and	mornings,"	which	some	read	as	1,150	full	days	if	each
evening	and	each	morning	is	counted	as	a	separate	half-unit.	The	verb	translated	"cleansed"	in	the
KJV	—	Hebrew	
nitsdaq
	—	comes	from	the	root	for	righteousness	or	justice,	not	from	a	word
meaning	ritual	cleansing;	other	translations	render	it	"restored	to	its	rightful	state"	or	"properly
restored."	Both	the	number	and	the	verb	are	genuinely	disputed	points	of	translation,	not	settled	ones.

--- PAGE 12 ---
Different	interpreters	have	understood	the	period	differently,	including:
2,300	prophetic	days
1,150	days,	based	on	understanding	the	expression	as	morning-and-evening	sacrifices	counted
separately
other	interpretations	connected	to	the	sanctuary	context
Historicist	interpreters	generally	understand	the	period	as	2,300	prophetic	days,	applying	the	day-year
principle	to	arrive	at	2,300	years,	and	generally	follow	the	KJV's	"cleansed"	in	connecting	the
passage	to	sanctuary	imagery.	That	interpretation	will	be	examined	later	in	the	course.
For	now,	the	methodological	point	is	enough:	Daniel	gives	the	number	and	the	verb;	historicist
interpretation	supplies	the	day-year	application,	a	translation	choice	on	the	verb,	and	the	historical
chronology	built	from	both.

PART	IX	—	THE	BIBLICAL	CASE	FOR	HISTORICISM
Why	This	Framework	Is	the	Correct	Reading	of	Daniel
Other	frameworks	exist.	We	named	them	so	you	will	not	be	surprised	when	you	meet	them.	But	this
course	does	not	treat	them	as	equally	faithful	to	Daniel.

--- PAGE 13 ---
Historicism	is	not	a	preference.	It	is	the	reading	that	best	fits	what	Daniel	actually	shows	—
when	Scripture	is	allowed	to	interpret	Scripture.
We	do	not	begin	with	a	church	slogan	and	then	hunt	for	verses.	We	begin	with	Daniel's	words,	then
let	Daniel's	parallel	visions,	the	Prophets,	and	the	New	Testament	explain	that	line.	That	is	the
Reformation	and	biblical	rule:	the	Word	explains	the	Word.
Here	is	the	biblical	case	—	built	by	Bible	study,	not	by	church	politics.
1.	Daniel	himself	gives	a	continuous	sequence,	not	a	single	ancient	crisis	and	not
a	blank	leap	to	the	end
In	Daniel	2	the	first	kingdom	is	named:	Babylon	—	"Thou	art	this	head	of	gold"	(Daniel	2:38).	Then
the	text	says:	"And	after	thee	shall	arise	another	kingdom	inferior	to	thee,	and	another	third
kingdom…	And	the	fourth	kingdom…"	(Daniel	2:39–40).	Then	feet	and	toes.	Then	God's	everlasting
kingdom	that	"shall	break	in	pieces	and	consume	all	these	kingdoms"	(Daniel	2:44).
TEXT:
	The	sequence	is	successive	("after	thee"),	multi-stage,	and	ends	only	when	God's	kingdom
replaces	the	whole	image.
INTERPRETATION	that	fits	the	text:
	Prophecy	here	tracks	a	line	of	empires	through	history	until
God's	final	kingdom.	That	is	historicism.
A	reading	that	dumps	almost	all	fulfillment	back	into	the	distant	past	(as	if	the	statue's	story	were
basically	over	in	antiquity)	struggles	to	honour	the	full	sequence	and	the	still-future	force	of	the
everlasting	kingdom	crushing	the	whole	image.
A	reading	that	parks	most	of	the	meaning	only	in	a	short	end-time	parenthesis	struggles	to	honour
the	plain	"after	thee"	succession	beginning	with	Babylon	in	Daniel's	own	day	and	running	through
multiple	kingdoms	before	the	end.
Historicism	takes	Daniel's	grammar	of	succession	seriously	from	start	to	finish.
2.	The	master	key:	Scripture	interprets	Scripture
This	is	the	non-negotiable	principle	behind	every	sheet.
Isaiah	teaches	that	God's	word	is	given	"precept	upon	precept;	line	upon	line"	(Isaiah	28:10).
Paul	teaches	that	we	compare	"spiritual	things	with	spiritual"	(1	Corinthians	2:13).
Jesus	Himself	"beginning	at	Moses	and	all	the	prophets…	expounded	unto	them	in	all	the
scriptures	the	things	concerning	himself"	(Luke	24:27).
Isaiah	8:20	sets	the	test:	"To	the	law	and	to	the	testimony:	if	they	speak	not	according	to	this
word,	it	is	because	there	is	no	light	in	them."
So	when	we	read	Daniel,	we	do	not	invent	a	private	code.	We	ask:	How	does	the	rest	of	Daniel
explain	this?	How	do	the	Prophets	speak?	How	does	Revelation	echo	Daniel?	How	does	Christ	fulfil
and	interpret	the	line?

--- PAGE 14 ---
2b.	Daniel's	visions	reinforce	one	progressive	line
Daniel	2,	7,	and	8	are	not	random	dreams.	They	rehearse	and	expand	the	same	kind	of	movement:
kingdoms	rise,	kingdoms	fall,	a	blasphemous	power	opposes	God	and	His	people,	and	God	wins.
TEXT:
	Repeated	parallel	structures	inside	one	book.
INTERPRETATION	that	fits:
	God	is	teaching	a	historical	line,	not	three	disconnected	puzzles.
Historicism	reads	the	parallels	as	one	unfolding	story.	That	is	how	careful	Bible	study	works	—
"comparing	spiritual	things	with	spiritual"	(1	Corinthians	2:13).
3.	The	everlasting	kingdom	is	God's	answer	inside	history's	arc	—	and	it	centres
in	Christ
Daniel	2:44	is	not	a	footnote.	It	is	the	destination	of	the	vision.	God	sets	up	a	kingdom	that	shall
never	be	destroyed.
The	New	Testament	identifies	Jesus	as	King	of	kings	(Revelation	17:14;	19:16)	and	teaches	that	the
Scriptures	concern	Him	(Luke	24:27,	44–47).	So	the	historicist	line	is	not	"history	for	history's	sake."
It	is	history	moving	toward	redemption	and	restoration	in	Christ.
If	prophecy	were	only	about	a	finished	ancient	crisis,	the	church	would	be	left	with	little	more	than	a
museum	tour.	If	prophecy	were	only	about	a	future	chart	disconnected	from	the	centuries	between
Babylon	and	the	end,	the	stone's	collision	with	the	whole	image	would	lose	the	force	Daniel	gives	it.
Historicism	keeps	both:	God	has	been	ruling	the	rise	and	fall	of	kingdoms,	and	Christ	is	the	goal.
4.	Prophetic	time	in	Daniel	is	tested	inside	the	book	—	and	historicism	passes	the
Daniel	9	test
Daniel	9	gives	"seventy	weeks"	concerning	Jerusalem,	Messiah,	and	covenant	(Daniel	9:24–27).
TEXT:
	Seventy	weeks	tied	to	Messiah	and	Jerusalem's	story.
INTERPRETATION:
	Historicist	Bible	study	applies	the	day-for-year	precedent	God	Himself	used
(Numbers	14:34;	Ezekiel	4:6)	and	then	tests	it	in	Daniel.	Four	hundred	ninety	literal	days	do	not
naturally	carry	the	Messiah-centred	history	Daniel	9	describes.	Four	hundred	ninety	years	do.	That	is
not	circular	slogan-shouting.	It	is:	Scripture	precedent	→	Daniel's	context	→	historical	fit	with	the
Messiah.
Later	lessons	will	walk	the	dates	carefully.	The	point	in	Sheet	0	is	methodological:	
Daniel's	own
time	prophecies	invite	an	extended	historical	reading
,	which	is	exactly	what	historicism	claims.
(Remember	the	honesty	rule:	Numbers	14	and	Ezekiel	4	are	precedent,	not	a	magic	wand.	Daniel	9	is
the	internal	test.	We	will	not	pretend	the	modern	dates	are	printed	in	the	verse.)

--- PAGE 15 ---
5.	Revelation	confirms	that	Daniel's	conflict	runs	through	Christian	history	to	the
end
Revelation	reuses	Daniel's	language	of	times,	beasts,	and	persecution	(for	example,	time	/	times	/	half
a	time	and	the	1,260	days	—	Revelation	12:6,	14;	13:5).	John	is	not	erasing	Daniel.	He	is	showing	the
same	war	continuing	until	the	Lamb	conquers.
INTERPRETATION	that	fits	both	Testaments:
	Prophecy	unfolds	across	the	age	between	the
prophets	and	the	final	victory	of	Christ.	That	is	historicism.	A	method	that	locks	Daniel	only	in	the
past,	or	only	in	a	brief	future	window,	must	explain	away	that	canonical	continuity.
6.	Jesus	taught	His	disciples	to	read	prophecy	toward	Himself	across	the	sweep
of	Scripture
"Beginning	at	Moses	and	all	the	prophets,	he	expounded	unto	them	in	all	the	scriptures	the	things
concerning	himself"	(Luke	24:27).	He	also	pointed	disciples	to	Daniel	in	the	context	of	coming	crisis
(Matthew	24:15).
Bible	study	under	Jesus'	own	method	is	not	allergy	to	history.	It	is	history	and	prophecy	ending	in
Christ.

PART	X	—	FOLLOWING	THE	CHAIN
Daniel	2	provides	the	first	major	sequence.	Daniel	identifies	Babylon.	Then:	"after	thee	shall	arise
another	kingdom…"	Then	another.	Then	another.	Finally,	God	establishes	a	kingdom	that	will	never
be	destroyed.	The	sequence	is	therefore	clear.	But	the	interpretation	must	still	be	demonstrated.

--- PAGE 16 ---
TEXT
	—	Daniel	explicitly	identifies	Babylon	and	describes	successive	kingdoms.
HISTORY
	—	Ancient	history	allows	us	to	investigate	which	empires	followed	Babylon.
INTERPRETATION
	—	Historicist	interpreters	identify	the	sequence	as:	Babylon	→	Medo-Persia
→	Greece	→	Rome	→	divided	political	world	→	God's	everlasting	kingdom.
The	first	four	kingdom	identifications	are	the	majority	reading	among	both	historicist	interpreters	and
most	confessional	scholarship	generally,	and	they	correlate	reasonably	well	with	Daniel's	later	visions
and	with	the	historical	record.	It	is	worth	knowing,	though,	that	not	every	reader	agrees	on	the	second
kingdom:	some	critical	scholarship	separates	"Media"	and	"Persia"	into	two	distinct	kingdoms	rather
than	one	combined	Medo-Persian	empire,	which	yields	a	different	four-kingdom	count	and	is	part	of
the	basis	for	reading	Daniel's	kingdoms	as	ending	with	Greece	rather	than	Rome	(a	position
associated	with	a	Maccabean	date	for	the	book's	composition,	discussed	further	under	Source
Checkpoint	08	below).	The	later	identification	of	the	divided	political	world,	and	the	exact	way	the
final	kingdom	relates	to	Christ's	return,	belong	to	the	interpretive	synthesis	and	will	be	examined
progressively.
THE	STONE
Daniel	describes	a	stone	"cut	out	without	hands."	It	strikes	the	image.	The	image	collapses.	The	stone
becomes	a	great	mountain.	And	"the	God	of	heaven	[shall]	set	up	a	kingdom,	which	shall	never	be
destroyed."	Daniel	explicitly	identifies	this	as	God's	everlasting	kingdom.
Within	the	wider	canonical	witness,	Christian	interpreters	understand	this	kingdom	in	relation	to
God's	final	reign	and,	ultimately,	to	Christ.	But	Daniel	2	itself	does	not	use	the	sentence	"The	stone	is
Jesus	Christ."	That	identification	emerges	through	the	wider	biblical	witness.	Therefore,	we	will
distinguish:
TEXT
	—	God	establishes	an	everlasting	kingdom.
CHRIST
	—	The	wider	New	Testament	identifies	Jesus	with	the	promised	King	and	everlasting
kingdom.
INTERPRETATION
	—	Christian	interpreters	therefore	understand	the	stone	and	its	kingdom	in
relation	to	Christ's	ultimate	reign.

	What	does	this	reveal	about	God's	kingdom	and	Christ?	Every

King.
PART	XII	—	CHRISTOLOGY:	REDEMPTION	AND
RESTORATION
The	Main	Narrative	Beneath	the	Kingdoms
Daniel	is	filled	with	kingdoms.	But	kingdoms	are	not	the	destination.
Babylon	rises.	Babylon	falls.	Persia	follows.	Greece	follows.	Rome	rises	and	changes.	Political
structures	come	and	go.	Human	rulers	die.
Daniel	keeps	pressing	toward	something	that	does	
not
	pass	away:	the	kingdom	the	God	of	heaven
sets	up,	which	shall	never	be	destroyed	(Daniel	2:44).
That	everlasting	kingdom	is	not	a	decorative	ending.	It	is	the	spine	of	the	book	—	and	it	belongs	to
the	wider	biblical	story	of	
redemption	and	restoration
.
1.	
God	rules	history.
	"He	removeth	kings,	and	setteth	up	kings"	(Daniel	2:21).	Exile	does	not	mean
God	lost	control.
2.	
God	redeems	His	people	in	the	middle	of	empire.
	Even	when	the	Lord	gave	Jehoiakim	into
Babylon's	hand	(Daniel	1:2),	He	also	gave	Daniel	favour	and	skill	(Daniel	1:9,	17).	Redemption	is
not	waiting	for	a	comfortable	century;	it	meets	people	in	captivity.
3.	
God	restores	what	sin	and	empire	cannot	restore.
	The	stone	becomes	a	mountain	filling	the
whole	earth	(Daniel	2:35).	The	kingdom	"shall	not	be	left	to	other	people"	(Daniel	2:44).
Restoration	is	God's	work,	not	Babylon's	rebranding.
4.	
Jesus	Christ	is	the	centre	of	that	hope.
	Jesus	taught	that	the	Scriptures	speak	of	Him	(Luke
24:27,	44–47).	He	is	"King	of	kings"	(Revelation	17:14;	19:16).	In	Him	we	have	"redemption
through	his	blood,	the	forgiveness	of	sins"	(Ephesians	1:7).	The	apostolic	witness	calls	people	to
repent	so	that	"times	of	refreshing"	and	the	restoration	promised	by	the	prophets	may	be	received
(Acts	3:19–21).	The	story	ends	not	in	endless	empire,	but	in	a	new	heaven	and	a	new	earth	where
God	dwells	with	His	people	(Revelation	21:1–5).

--- PAGE 18 ---
So	the	main	narrative	is	this:
	Prophecy	is	not	empire	trivia.	It	is	the	unveiling	of	God's	redeeming
rule	and	His	restoring	kingdom	in	Christ.	Every	sheet	in	this	course	should	leave	you	nearer	Jesus	—
crucified,	risen,	and	returning	as	King	—	not	merely	nearer	a	chart.
In	one	sentence:
	We	trace	kingdoms	so	we	can	trust	the	Redeemer	who	restores	all	things	under	His
everlasting	reign.
PART	XIII	—	A	PERSONAL	NOTE
For	Your	Life,	Not	Only	for	Ancient	History
Daniel's	crisis	was	public.	Yours	may	be	private.
You	may	never	stand	in	Nebuchadnezzar's	court.	But	you	may	live	under	pressure	that	tries	to	rename
you,	reshape	your	desires,	and	rewrite	your	loyalties.
Use	the	same	four	questions	on	your	own	life	this	week:
1.	
TEXT
	—	What	has	God	actually	said	in	His	Word	about	who	you	are,	and	about	Jesus?
2.	
HISTORY
	—	What	are	the	real	facts	of	your	situation	—	not	rumours,	not	panic?
3.	
INTERPRETATION
	—	Where	might	you	be	stacking	fear,	pride,	or	someone	else's	theory	on
top	of	Scripture?
4.	
CHRIST
	—	How	is	Jesus	inviting	you	to	receive	His	redemption	today,	and	to	hope	in	His

You	do	not	need	a	secret	timeline	to	begin.	You	need	a	clear	Word,	an	honest	heart,	and	a	living

Pray:
	"Lord	Jesus,	redeem	what	is	broken	in	me.	Restore	what	I	cannot	fix.	Teach	me	to	read	Your

SOURCE	08	—	JEROME	AND	PORPHYRY
	—	Jerome	preserves	an	important	ancient	debate
concerning	Daniel's	interpretation.	Porphyry	argued	that	Daniel's	prophecies	were	composed	in	the
Maccabean	period	and	primarily	described	events	surrounding	Antiochus	IV.	Jerome	opposed	this
interpretation.	Porphyry's	original	work	does	not	survive	independently	—	it	is	known	
chiefly
through	Jerome's
	quotations	and	rebuttals,	which	is	itself	worth	noting	when	weighing	how	fully	we
can	reconstruct	his	argument.	This	is	important	because	it	demonstrates	that	debates	about	the
historical	location	of	Daniel's	fulfillment	are	ancient.	It	also	shows	why	arguments	about	Daniel's
date	and	fulfillment	must	be	examined	separately.
SOURCE	09	—	HIPPOLYTUS
	—	Hippolytus	provides	early	Christian	evidence	for	identifying	the
kingdoms	in	Daniel's	visions	with	a	sequence	commonly	understood	as	Babylon	→	Persia	→	Greece
→	Rome.	This	is	valuable	historical	evidence	concerning	early	Christian	interpretation.	It's	worth
knowing,	though,	that	Hippolytus	also	expected	a	future	individual	antichrist	figure	and	a	literal,
future	3½-year	period	—	elements	that	later	interpreters	would	associate	more	with	futurism	than
with	historicism.	Early	interpreters	did	not	sort	neatly	into	the	later	categories;	Hippolytus	is	evidence
for	the	antiquity	of	the	four-kingdom	sequence	specifically,	not	for	a	fully	formed	historicist	system.
That	distinction	matters.

WHY	THIS	MATTERS
Prophecy	can	become	dangerous	when	interpretation	is	hidden.	A	teacher	can	present	an
interpretation	as	though	it	were	a	quotation	from	Scripture.	A	historical	reconstruction	can	be
presented	as	though	it	were	explicitly	stated	by	Daniel.	A	theological	conclusion	can	be	presented	as
though	there	were	no	alternative	readings.	That	is	not	careful	study.
A	trustworthy	Bible	study	should	allow	the	student	to	see	the	evidence	trail.	That	is	why	this	course
will	repeatedly	distinguish	what	Scripture	says,	what	history	documents,	what	the	interpretation
concludes,	and	what	the	biblical	story	reveals	about	Christ.
CHRIST	AT	THE	CENTER
Daniel	is	filled	with	kingdoms.	But	kingdoms	are	not	the	destination.	Babylon	disappears.	Persia
disappears.	Greece	disappears.	Rome	changes.	Political	structures	rise	and	fall.	Human	rulers	die.
But	Daniel	ends	with	something	different.	An	everlasting	kingdom.	A	kingdom	that	does	not	pass	to
another	people.	A	kingdom	established	by	God.
This	is	where	prophecy	moves	beyond	political	history.	It	moves	toward	hope	—	toward	redemption
and	restoration.	The	question	is	no	longer	simply	"Who	ruled	Babylon?"	The	deeper	question
becomes:	Who	will	rule	when	every	earthly	kingdom	has	passed	away?
Daniel's	answer	is	the	kingdom	of	God.	And	the	New	Testament	presents	Jesus	Christ	as	the	King	at
the	centre	of	God's	redemptive	kingdom.
Prophecy	therefore	should	not	merely	make	us	knowledgeable	about	history.	It	should	lead	us	to	trust
the	God	who	holds	history	in	His	hands	—	and	the	Christ	who	redeems	and	restores.
QUESTIONS	FOR	THE	STUDENT
1.	What	is	hermeneutics?
Hermeneutics	is	the	discipline	of	interpreting	texts.	In	biblical	prophecy,	it	concerns	how	we
understand	Scripture,	symbols,	context,	chronology,	and	fulfillment.
2.	What	four	levels	will	this	course	use	when	studying	prophecy?
TEXT	—	what	Scripture	explicitly	says.	HISTORY	—	what	historical	evidence	documents.
INTERPRETATION	—	what	conclusion	the	interpretive	framework	draws.	CHRIST	—	how	the
wider	biblical	witness	directs	us	toward	Christ.
3.	What	does	Daniel	1:2	explicitly	say	about	Jehoiakim?
It	says	that	the	Lord	gave	Jehoiakim	king	of	Judah	into	Nebuchadnezzar's	hand.
4.	What	historical	date	is	traditionally	associated	with	Daniel's	first	Babylonian	captivity,	and
why	does	Jeremiah	give	a	different	year?
Traditional	chronological	reconstructions	commonly	place	it	around	605	B.C.	Daniel	dates	it	to
Jehoiakim's	third	year,	while	Jeremiah	dates	the	same	events	to	his	fourth	year	(Jeremiah	25:1;	46:2).
A	standard	scholarly	resolution	is	that	the	two	books	use	different	regnal-year	counting	conventions

--- PAGE 21 ---
(Babylonian	accession-year	reckoning	versus	a	non-accession-year	system);	other	calendar	models
also	exist.	The	BCE	date	itself	is	reconstructed	from	broader	biblical	and	ancient	chronological
evidence,	not	stated	as	a	calendar	year	in	the	text.
5.	What	is	Historicism?
Historicism	is	an	interpretive	framework	that	understands	biblical	prophecy	as	unfolding
progressively	through	history,	often	identifying	prophetic	sequences	with	successive	historical
developments.
6.	Did	every	Reformer	hold	exactly	the	same	prophetic	interpretation?
No.	Interpreters	before	and	during	the	Reformation	(including	Wycliffe	as	a	precursor,	and	Reformers
such	as	Luther,	Melanchthon,	and	Knox)	used	historical	approaches	to	prophecy,	but	their
interpretations	differed	in	important	details.	Historicist	
tendencies
	are	not	the	same	as	one	uniform
system.
7.	What	are	Preterism	and	Futurism?
Preterist	approaches	generally	locate	major	prophetic	fulfillment	primarily	in	the	ancient	historical
setting,	while	futurist	approaches	understand	significant	portions	of	prophecy	as	awaiting	future
fulfillment.	Each	school	has	internal	varieties.
8.	What	do	Numbers	14:34	and	Ezekiel	4:6	demonstrate	—	and	what	don't	they?
They	demonstrate	that	Scripture	can	explicitly	use	a	day-for-year	relationship	in	symbolic	contexts.
They	do	not	establish	that	every	prophetic	day	in	every	biblical	passage	must	equal	one	year,	and
neither	passage	is	itself	a	predictive	time-prophecy	—	Numbers	14:34	is	a	judgment	announced	after
the	fact,	and	Ezekiel	4:6	is	a	commanded	sign-act.
9.	How	do	historicist	interpreters	use	Daniel	9:24–27	in	relation	to	prophetic	time?
They	understand	the	seventy	weeks	as	490	prophetic	units	and,	applying	the	day-year	principle,
interpret	them	as	490	years.	They	then	compare	that	chronology	with	biblical	and	historical	evidence
concerning	Jerusalem	and	the	Messiah.
10.	What	is	the	purpose	of	distinguishing	Text,	History,	and	Interpretation?
It	prevents	us	from	confusing	what	Scripture	explicitly	states	with	historical	evidence	and	theological
conclusions	derived	from	an	interpretive	framework	—	including	cases,	like	the	1,260-day	figure,
where	several	interpretive	steps	are	stacked	on	top	of	one	another	and	can	be	mistaken	for	a	single
plain	statement	of	Scripture.
11.	What	is	the	main	narrative	of	Daniel	beyond	the	rise	and	fall	of	empires?
Redemption	and	restoration	under	God's	everlasting	kingdom,	centred	in	Jesus	Christ	(see	Part	XIII).

The	prophetic	story	begins	with	a	young	man	surrounded	by	a	culture	determined	to	reshape	him.
