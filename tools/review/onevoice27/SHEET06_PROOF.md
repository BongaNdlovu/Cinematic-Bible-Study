## 1. The Historical Coordination of "Darius the Mede"

### The Contested Claim
Critics argue that "Darius the Mede" (Dan 5:31, 6:1, 9:1, 11:1) is a historical error conflating the Persian king Darius I Hystaspes (522–486 B.C.) with the fall of Babylon in 539 B.C.

### Biblical & Historical Resolution
1. **Daniel's Precise Textual Distinction:**
   - Daniel 5:31 explicitly states Darius was "about threescore and two years old" (approx. 62 years old) when he took the kingdom. Darius I Hystaspes was a young man in 522 B.C., not an elderly man of 62 in 539 B.C.
   - Daniel 9:1 says Darius the Mede was "made king over the realm of the Chaldeans" (passive verb *homlak*, indicating he was appointed or installed over the sub-realm, not the supreme monarch of the entire Persian empire).
   - Daniel 6:28 coordinates the reigns: "So this Daniel prospered in the reign of Darius, and in the reign of Cyrus the Persian" (or "even the reign of Cyrus the Persian", where the *waw* can be explicative).

2. **Primary Witnesses:**
   - **Xenophon, *Cyropaedia* (Book I, 5.2; Book VIII, 5.19):**
     Documents that Cyrus the Great took Babylon in joint alliance with his uncle Cyaxares II, son of Astyages king of Media. Xenophon states that Cyrus gave the royal palace in Babylon to Cyaxares II and married his daughter. Many historicists and conservative historians coordinate Darius the Mede with Cyaxares II.
   - **The Nabonidus Chronicle (BM 35382, Col. III, lines 15–20):**
     Records that upon the capture of Babylon by Ugbaru (governor of Gutium) on the 16th of Tashritu (Oct 12, 539 B.C.), Cyrus entered on the 3rd of Arahsamnu (Oct 29), and Gubaru appointed governors (*pihat*) in Babylon. Other scholars (e.g. William H. Shea, John C. Whitcomb) identify Darius the Mede with Gubaru, whom Cyrus placed over the province of Babylon.
   - **Flavius Josephus, *Antiquities of the Jews* (Book X, Chapter 11, Section 4):**
     > "Darius, who, in conjunction with his relation Cyrus, had put an end to the dominion of the Babylonians, was sixty-two years old when he took Babylon... he was the son of Astyages, and had another name among the Greeks."
   - Conclusion: Daniel's narrative fits established Persian-Median dual-monarchy governance; the sitting carefully frames this as historical coordination backed by ancient records.
